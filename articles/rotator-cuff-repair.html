Rotator Cuff Tears and Repair

Degenerative and traumatic cuff tears: when to treat non-operatively, how arthroscopic repair works, and what to do with irreparable tears.

RegionShoulder
TypeCondition
Updated2026-01-12
Reading time2 min
Connected22 articles

Overview

Rotator cuff tears are a leading cause of shoulder pain and weakness in adults. Most involve the supraspinatus and are degenerative, although acute traumatic tears occur in younger patients after a fall or dislocation. Many tears, including some full-thickness tears, stay quiet or settle without surgery.

Presentation and indications

  • Lateral shoulder pain, night pain and weakness of elevation or external rotation.
  • Examination: painful arc, drop-arm or external rotation lag signs, supraspinatus and infraspinatus strength testing.
  • Ultrasound or MRI defines tear size, retraction and fatty infiltration of the muscle.
  • Surgery is favoured for acute traumatic tears, active patients with full-thickness tears, and failure of structured non-operative care.

Management

  • Non-operative: activity modification, targeted physiotherapy, analgesia and selective injection.
  • Arthroscopic repair with suture anchors, single-row or double-row, aiming for a tension-free footprint repair.
  • Partial repair, superior capsular reconstruction or tendon transfer for large tears that cannot be repaired.
  • Reverse shoulder arthroplasty when the cuff is irreparable and the joint is arthritic or pseudoparalytic.

Outcomes and pitfalls

Pain relief is reliable after repair. Healing is less certain: re-tear risk rises with tear size, age, smoking and fatty infiltration. Stiffness is the commonest early complication, so the rehabilitation plan has to match the quality of the repair.

Key points

  • Tear size and tissue quality drive healing, not just surgical technique.
  • Many degenerative tears do well without surgery.
  • Irreparable tears need a different strategy, not a stronger repair.
