Brachial Plexus Injuries

Mechanisms, root avulsion, timing of exploration and a reconstructive ladder from nerve transfer to functional muscle transfer.

RegionReconstruction
TypeCondition
Updated2026-09-01
Reading time2 min
Connected21 articles

Overview

Brachial plexus injuries range from stretch neuropraxia to complete root avulsion. Closed traction injuries after motorcycle or high-energy trauma are the most common in adults; obstetric injuries occur at birth.

Assessment

  • Map the level of injury by muscle group and sensory loss.
  • Horner syndrome suggests a proximal lower trunk or root avulsion.
  • MRI or CT myelography, plus electrodiagnostics from about three to four weeks.
  • Look for associated vascular and bony injury.

Reconstruction

  • Priorities: elbow flexion, shoulder stability and abduction, hand sensation, then prehension.
  • Exploration and nerve grafting or transfer when there is no recovery after three to six months.
  • Tendon transfers and free functional muscle transfer for late cases.
  • Shoulder arthrodesis or palliative procedures when reconstruction is not possible.

Pitfalls

The time window for motor reinnervation is limited. Pain from root avulsion can be severe and needs a dedicated pain strategy. Realistic goal setting is essential.

Key points

  • Timing governs motor recovery.
  • Avulsed roots need transfers, not grafts.
  • Rehabilitation continues for years.
