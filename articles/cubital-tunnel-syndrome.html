Cubital Tunnel Syndrome

Ulnar nerve compression at the elbow: when to splint, and decompression versus transposition.

RegionElbow
TypeCondition
Updated2026-06-08
Reading time2 min
Connected18 articles

Overview

Cubital tunnel syndrome is the second most common compression neuropathy of the upper limb after carpal tunnel syndrome. The ulnar nerve is compressed or stretched behind the medial epicondyle, particularly with sustained elbow flexion.

Presentation

  • Tingling in the small finger and ulnar half of the ring finger, worse with elbow flexion.
  • Weak pinch and clumsiness; later, intrinsic wasting, clawing and Froment sign.
  • Tinel sign at the elbow and positive elbow flexion test.
  • Nerve conduction studies help grade severity, though they can be normal early.

Treatment

  • Mild or intermittent symptoms: avoid prolonged flexion and leaning on the elbow, night extension splint.
  • Surgery for persistent or progressive symptoms, or motor loss.
  • In situ decompression, open or endoscopic, is as effective as transposition for straightforward cases.
  • Anterior transposition or medial epicondylectomy for subluxing nerve or revision.

Pitfalls

Medial antebrachial cutaneous branches cross the incision and a neuroma can be a source of persistent pain. Long-standing compression with muscle wasting recovers incompletely.

Key points

  • Do not wait for wasting before offering surgery.
  • In situ decompression is often enough.
  • Protect cutaneous branches.
