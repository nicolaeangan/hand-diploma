Reverse Total Shoulder Arthroplasty

How the reverse design restores elevation without a working cuff, who it suits, and the complications to plan for.

RegionShoulder
TypeProcedure
Updated2026-01-26
Reading time2 min
Connected13 articles

Overview

In a reverse prosthesis the ball is fixed to the glenoid and the socket to the humerus. This medialises and lowers the centre of rotation and lengthens the deltoid lever arm, so the deltoid can elevate the arm when the rotator cuff cannot.

Indications

  • Cuff tear arthropathy and irreparable cuff tears with pseudoparalysis.
  • Complex proximal humerus fractures in older patients.
  • Osteoarthritis with glenoid bone loss or an insufficient cuff.
  • Revision of failed anatomic arthroplasty or hemiarthroplasty.

Technique

  • Deltopectoral approach, subscapularis managed according to surgeon preference and implant.
  • Glenoid baseplate placed with secure fixation and attention to inferior tilt and overhang.
  • Humeral stem cemented or press-fit, with version and soft-tissue tension set to balance stability and motion.
  • A functioning deltoid and axillary nerve are prerequisites.

Outcomes and pitfalls

Pain relief and elevation are generally good. Dislocation, scapular notching, acromial or scapular spine stress fracture, infection and loosening are the main concerns. Rotation, particularly internal rotation, is often limited.

Key points

  • Success depends on the deltoid, not the cuff.
  • Component positioning governs stability and notching.
  • Counsel on limited internal rotation and activity limits.
